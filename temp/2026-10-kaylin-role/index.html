Head of Research and Insight

You lead the research behind every Commercial Foundation: customer interviews, market and competitor research, and the evidence of why buyers choose, hesitate or walk away. What you find is what the positioning, the message and the creative are built on.

Why this role matters here

Most agencies guess what a customer wants, then design something beautiful around the guess. We do it the other way round. Every client starts with a Commercial Foundation, and the heart of it is simple: the reason customers should choose a business comes from its customers. We hear it from them before anything is designed, written or advertised.

Everything the rest of the team makes stands on what you bring back from the field.

So this is not a support role. If the research is thin, the brand is a guess with good typography. If it is sharp, the copywriter, the designer and the ad buyer all know exactly what to say and to whom.

To be straight with you: this is the first time we are putting a dedicated person on it. You would be building how we do research, not slotting into a routine someone else set up.

What you will actually do

Talk to people

Customer interviews and field surveys

Short, structured conversations with customers, would-be customers, staff and people who said no. In person, on the phone or on a video call. Most of them ten minutes or less.

Look around

Market and competitor research

Who else is out there, what they promise, what they charge, what their customers complain about in reviews, and where the gap is.

Make sense of it

Turn answers into insight

Group what people said, count it, pull out the exact phrases they use, and say plainly how sure we are. "Seven of twelve said..." beats "people feel...".

Hand it on

Feed the team

Write up findings so strategy, copy and design can use them the same week. Your quotes often end up as the headline, word for word.

Alongside that, the careful part of the job:

  • Consent, kept natural. People are only included once they are happy to chat, nobody under 18 takes part, and nothing is recorded unless they say yes. It should feel like a friendly conversation, never a form.
  • Records that we can use later. How long each conversation took, and every "no thanks" with the reason they gave. That tells us how long research really takes, so we can quote it properly.
  • Keeping people's details safe. Names and numbers are personal information under POPIA. You will sign a short confidentiality agreement with us before you collect any.

Three examples of the work

Example 1 · Starting this month

Bonga: tipping for car guards and other informal workers

Bonga is a new way to tip people like car guards, petrol attendants and golf caddies without cash, built so the worker does not need a bank account. Before we build the brand, we need to hear from the people who would use it.

Who you would talk to, anywhere from George to Plett: 20 to 30 car guards, 20 to 25 people who tip car guards, and around 10 to 15 each of petrol attendants, forecourt customers, golf caddies and golfers.

What we need to learn: how often people tip now and how much, what stops them (often simply "I had no change"), how the workers feel about being tipped digitally, and whether anyone already uses something similar.

Example 2 · Starting this month

QR-TIP: tipping in hotels, lodges, restaurants and spas

QR-TIP lets guests tip staff by scanning a code, with the money going straight to the staff member. We need the view from both sides of the counter.

Who you would talk to, across four markets (restaurants, salons and spas, game lodges, hotels): for each, roughly 10 to 15 staff, 6 to 8 owners or managers, and 20 guests.

What we need to learn: how tips reach staff today, where they get lost or held back, what owners worry about, and whether guests ever leave without tipping because they have no cash.

Example 3 · A typical client

"We get leads, but they don't buy"

A business comes to us with plenty of enquiries and too few signed deals. Instead of rewriting their website on a hunch, you call two or three prospects who said no, a few current clients (a busy one, a quiet one, and one who left) and some live prospects.

What they tell you becomes the new pitch, the sales scripts and the follow-up. Often the answer is something the business never suspected, like buyers not understanding what they get in the first month.

What a good question looks like

People are poor at predicting what they would do and very good at remembering what they did. So we ask about the last real time, never about a made-up future.

Avoid"Would you use an app to tip car guards?"
Ask instead"Think about the last time you parked at a shopping centre. Did you tip the car guard? What happened?"
Avoid"How much would you pay for this?"
Ask instead"What did you last tip, and how did you decide on that amount?"
Avoid"Don't you think tipping by phone is easier?"
Ask instead"Has there been a time you wanted to tip but couldn't? Tell me about it."

Then you listen, and write down their words exactly as they said them. "I never have coins on me anymore" is worth more to us than a neat summary.

What a field day might look like

  • 08:30Petrol station before the morning rush is over. Three quick conversations with customers after they have paid, two with attendants when it is quiet.
  • 10:30Shopping centre car park. Car guards first, always away from any supervisor. Then shoppers once they have parked and walked away from the guard.
  • 13:00A hotel. Ask at the front desk for ten minutes with the manager, at a time that suits them. Catch staff on their break, out of earshot of management.
  • 15:00Golf club. Caddies between rounds, golfers in the clubhouse.
  • 17:00Quick notes: anything surprising, any pattern starting to show, how many said no and why.

Every answer goes into a questionnaire on your phone. Short answers are tapped in. For the longer answers you press the microphone, read the question, let them talk, and press again to save the recording against that question. The time each conversation takes is recorded automatically.

The ground rules

  • Research is never selling. You do not pitch the product during a conversation, and you do not need to name it.
  • No one under 18.
  • Workers are protected. Staff and workers are spoken to away from their managers, and nothing they say is ever linked to them in anything their employer sees.
  • No guilt. Nobody is made to feel bad for not tipping, or for saying no to you.
  • Fees and partners stay private. If someone asks what it costs or who is behind it, you say it is early research and the details are not public yet.
  • Ten minutes means ten minutes. If someone is busy, you stop, thank them, and move on.

Who does well in this role

  • You are genuinely curious about people and comfortable walking up to a stranger.
  • You can make anyone feel at ease, from a car guard to a lodge owner, and you can switch language when it helps.
  • You listen more than you talk, and you notice the thing someone said almost in passing.
  • You are organised: you keep track of who you spoke to, what they agreed to, and what is still outstanding.
  • You can explain what you found in plain words, without dressing it up.
